Section I: Strand One – Knowledge
Strand Reflection
Throughout the Knowledge strand, I have developed an integrated understanding of research paradigms, theoretical frameworks, and methodological design within applied educational and professional practice. My learning has centered on connecting epistemology to actionable inquiry, ensuring that research contributes meaningfully to problem solving and innovation. Early in the program, coursework on paradigms and theoretical perspectives helped me examine how knowledge claims are justified, leading to my selection of Pragmatism as my philosophical foundation. Pragmatism emphasizes the usefulness of knowledge—truth as what works in context—which resonates with my professional focus on human–wildlife conflict resolution and digital governance.
Building upon this foundation, my work in Design Science Research (DSR) clarified how applied design processes can serve as a form of scholarly inquiry. Through my Methodology Concept Paper and Research Design submission (Chapters 1–3), I learned to connect theory, design, and evaluation in ways that prioritize both innovation and practical outcomes. Courses in research design and systems thinking reinforced this understanding by encouraging methodological congruence and theoretical coherence. I also refined my analytical and writing skills, aligning them with APA 7 standards and scholarly expectations.
This strand has culminated in a robust understanding of how Socio-Technical Systems Theory can serve as an applied lens for pragmatic research. By examining how technological and human systems co-evolve, I have grounded my dissertation’s focus on designing and recommending a prototype for human–wildlife interaction and conflict resolution. My Knowledge strand thus represents the intellectual scaffolding that enables me to connect philosophical assumptions, theoretical frameworks, and methodological execution into a coherent scholarly identity.

Section II: Strand Two – Practice
Strand Reflection
The Practice strand has been the arena where theoretical learning transformed into professional innovation. My experience working with the Wildlife of Africa Conservation Initiative and the WA BiCC Program provided authentic contexts for applying design thinking, systems governance, and digital transformation strategies. Through coursework and professional projects, I learned to integrate research findings with practical interventions that address community-based conservation challenges.
The Professional Innovation Design Project served as a key artifact in this strand, allowing me to prototype a digital governance system for human–wildlife conflict mitigation. Through this project, I applied Design Science Research principles to create and refine a socio-technical framework capable of supporting decision-making and collaboration in real time. My practice-focused artifacts demonstrate a sustained commitment to using evidence-informed strategies and stakeholder collaboration to address complex ecological and social issues.
In this strand, I also deepened my skills in leadership, facilitation, and cross-sectoral governance. By examining how digital platforms can serve as mechanisms for inclusive participation, I have expanded my understanding of educational and civic leadership in the context of sustainability and ethical innovation. These experiences directly inform my dissertation’s emphasis on Pragmatism and Socio-Technical Systems Theory, ensuring that my design recommendations are grounded in lived practice and responsive to community realities.

Section III: Strand Three – Self-in-Context
Strand Reflection
The Self-in-Context strand has been transformative, prompting deep reflection on identity, ethics, and purpose as a scholar-practitioner. Through engagement with ecological ethics, reflective leadership, and positionality, I have developed a clearer sense of how personal values shape research and practice. This strand has helped me internalize ethical responsibility, ecological awareness, and cultural humility as essential components of educational and professional practice.
My Environmental Ethics Paper, exploring Deep Ecology, Ecofeminism, and the Land Ethic, provided the conceptual grounding for my evolving ecological worldview. I came to see that ethical leadership requires systems awareness and a commitment to justice not only among people but also within the more-than-human world. This realization complements the Pragmatic foundation of my dissertation, where “what works” must always include what sustains life and community.
The Week 13 Observation and Reflection Paper from the Virtual Human–Wildlife Conflict Workshop allowed me to connect my leadership practice to reflective inquiry, emphasizing empathy, dialogue, and inclusivity. In my Professional Reflection and Leadership Philosophy Statement, I articulated a model of leadership that integrates ecological consciousness, technological responsibility, and collaborative governance. Together, these artifacts demonstrate my evolving self-understanding as a reflective, ethical innovator—one who seeks solutions that honor human and ecological well-being.
